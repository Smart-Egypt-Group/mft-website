Internal audit

Continuous auditing with AI vs traditional audit: side by side

A traditional audit looks back at a closed year through a sample. Continuous auditing tests every transaction as it posts. Here is what changes, and what does not.

Internal auditBy Ahmed Ashraf Mostafa14 October 20267 min read

Reviewed by MFT audit and Odoo practicelast reviewed 14 October 2026

Short answer

Continuous auditing with AI tests every transaction against defined rules shortly after it is posted, instead of testing a sample of a closed period months later. It shortens the time between an error and its detection and extends coverage to the whole population. It does not replace the external auditor’s opinion, professional judgement, or a named approver for each finding.

Key takeaways

  • The real difference is detection lag: months after the close versus days after posting.
  • Rules catch known patterns, models rank what rules miss, and a person decides every finding.
  • Continuous auditing sees only what was recorded, so physical counts, confirmations and judgement stay human.
  • Start with one process, three to five tests and a named reviewer, then widen.

A finance manager receives the management letter in April. One point says a vendor was paid twice for the same invoice the previous May. The payment is eleven months old, the person who approved it has changed roles, and the question in the board meeting is not what happened but why nobody saw it for a year. Nothing in that audit was done badly. It was done late, by design.

That design is the subject of this comparison. The ACFE’s 2026 Report to the Nations found that the median occupational fraud lasts 12 months before it is detected, and that tips, not audits, were the top detection method in 43% of cases. The case for testing all year rather than once is made in why an annual audit misses eleven months; this article takes the AI angle and puts the two approaches side by side, so you can decide what to keep, what to change and in what order.

What is continuous auditing with AI?

Continuous auditing with AI is the practice of testing every ledger transaction against defined audit rules shortly after it is posted, using automated tests and AI models to rank risk, and routing each exception to a named person who reviews it against evidence. Two words carry the weight: every, and named. Coverage becomes the full population; accountability stays with a person.

It helps to separate it from continuous monitoring. Monitoring is owned by management and is part of the control itself, for example a payables supervisor who reviews a daily duplicate report. Auditing is owned by an independent function, internal audit or an outsourced provider, that tests whether those controls work. Both fit COSO’s Principle 16, which calls for ongoing and separate evaluations of internal control. For the wider map of where AI sits in finance, start with our practical guide to AI in accounting.

How does continuous auditing differ from a traditional audit?

Traditional audit and continuous auditing with AI, compared on the dimensions that change the outcome
DimensionTraditional auditContinuous auditing with AI
TimingAfter the period closes, once or a few times a yearDaily or after each posting run, throughout the year
CoverageA sample selected under ISA 530, plus targeted itemsEvery transaction in the defined population, ranked by risk
Detection lagMonths: the error is found after the books are closedDays: the error is found while the period is still open
EvidenceDocuments requested and inspected during fieldworkLedger records with their ids, linked to source documents and stored with the finding
OutputAn opinion (external) or a periodic report (internal)A live exception queue, with periodic reports built from it
Who decidesThe audit team, at the end of fieldworkA named reviewer per exception, escalated by materiality
Main weaknessSampling risk and stale findingsAlert fatigue, data quality and the quality of the test design

Continuous auditing does not remove risk; it exchanges one kind for another. Sampling risk shrinks for the attributes you test, because nothing is left out, a shift explained in detail in our guide to moving from sampling to full-population testing. In its place come the risks of a badly designed test, an incomplete extract and a reviewer who stops reading alerts.

An external auditor still forms an opinion under the ISAs and decides how far to rely on anyone else’s work. Better internal evidence makes that reliance easier to justify, not automatic. The standards themselves are moving: in August 2026 the IAASB issued exposure drafts revising ISA 500, ISA 330 and ISA 520 to address the increased use of technology, with comments due by 15 December 2026. They are drafts, so do not build methodology on wording that may change.

What does AI actually do in a continuous audit?

Three layers do different jobs, and confusing them is a frequent design mistake.

Rules: the known patterns

Deterministic tests answer yes or no questions and run first, because they are cheap, explainable and repeatable. The patterns we find most often in continuous-audit work are:

  • Duplicate and near-duplicate payments to the same vendor.
  • Near-duplicate vendors: the same supplier set up twice under slightly different names.
  • Approvals outside the approval matrix, including approvals by the person who raised the request, a test that depends on clean segregation of duties, including AI agents as users.
  • Cut-off errors, where the document date and the posting period disagree.
  • Inventory valuation drift between the valuation report and the ledger.
  • Manual journal entries posted late at month-end in round amounts, the population ISA 240 expects auditors to examine when testing journal entries.

Models: what rules miss

A rule finds an exact duplicate. A model finds the bill that is not identical to anything but is unusual in several ways at once: a new vendor, an unusual posting hour, a user who rarely posts to that account. The output is a ranking, not a verdict. Its value is that the reviewer reads the riskiest twenty exceptions first rather than the first twenty.

Language: explaining and drafting

Language models are good at turning an exception into a readable draft finding and at pulling the related documents together. They are also capable of stating a number that is not in the books. The control is simple and non-negotiable: every statement in a draft must cite the records it came from, or it is rejected. We call that rule evidence or silence, explained in our article on AI provenance and the audit trail. And a flag is a question, not a conclusion; AI-based fraud detection explains why a red flag is never proof of fraud.

What does a continuous audit cycle look like in practice?

  1. Extract new postings since the last run through a dedicated read-only API user, as described in connecting AI to Odoo for continuous auditing.
  2. Reconcile the extract to the trial balance for the same period, so you know the population is complete before you test it.
  3. Run the rule tests, then the model scoring, on the new population.
  4. Suppress only patterns that have been explained before, and record who approved each suppression and why.
  5. Assign every remaining exception to a named reviewer, who checks it against the source document.
  6. Raise a finding with condition, criteria, cause, owner and due date, or close the exception with the evidence that explains it.
  7. Draft any correction for a person to approve, with a second approver above the ISA 320 materiality threshold, as set out in our note on materiality thresholds.
  8. Report monthly: open findings, ageing, repeats of earlier findings, and tests that produced nothing (which may mean the test is broken).

Where does the traditional audit still win?

A continuous test sees only what was recorded. It cannot count stock in a warehouse, obtain a bank confirmation, read the tone of a management meeting, or see a side agreement that was never entered. Accounting estimates, going concern, related parties outside the books and the plausibility of management’s explanations remain judgement work. The ACFE’s 2026 report also found that more than half of cases involved a lack of internal controls or an override of existing ones, and an override performed by someone with the right access can look perfectly normal in the data.

There is also a dependency the traditional auditor checks first and a continuous programme often forgets: the IT environment. ISA 315 (Revised 2019) requires auditors to understand general IT controls because ledger data is only as reliable as the access rights behind it. Odoo’s 20 release notes say changes to user groups are now logged in the user’s chatter, which gives a reviewer a trail of who received which access and when. Test that trail before you trust the transactions.

What are the risks, and how do you control them?

  • Alert fatigue. Control: thresholds linked to materiality, and a weekly review of the ten most suppressed patterns.
  • Incomplete data. Control: reconcile every extract to the trial balance and stop the run if it does not agree.
  • Silent test failure. Control: a test that returns zero exceptions for three cycles is reviewed, not celebrated.
  • Loss of independence. Control: the people who own the process do not edit the tests, and every rule change is logged with an approver.
  • Invented explanations. Control: drafts without record citations are rejected and never presented as facts.
  • Usage cost. If you use Odoo’s native AI, Odoo’s 20 release notes say all AI features consume IAP credits billed per request, so budget by volume of runs.

A finding that arrives eleven months late is a history lesson, not a control.

Where should a finance team start on Monday?

Pick one process with high volume and clear rules, usually payables. Choose three to five tests from the list above. Run them backwards on the last closed quarter and compare the results with what your last audit sample found; that comparison calibrates thresholds and tells you which tests produce noise. Then run weekly, assign a named reviewer, and keep the working papers ISA 230 expects: what was tested, what was found and how each exception was resolved. Widen to a second process only when the first queue is being cleared on time.

Questions people ask

Does continuous auditing replace the external auditor?

No. The external auditor still forms an independent opinion under the ISAs and decides how much to rely on internal work. Continuous auditing improves the evidence available during the year and can reduce surprises at year end, but reliance is the external auditor’s judgement, not something the company can assume.

What is the difference between continuous auditing and continuous monitoring?

Continuous monitoring is performed by management as part of a control, such as a supervisor reviewing a daily exceptions report. Continuous auditing is performed by an independent function that tests whether those controls work. Both use similar automated tests; the difference is ownership and independence.

Which transactions should be tested continuously first?

Start where volume is high and rules are clear: vendor bills and payments, manual journal entries, and vendor master changes. These produce testable patterns such as duplicates, approvals outside the matrix and late round-amount entries, and errors there move cash out of the business quickly.

Can AI-flagged exceptions be used as audit evidence?

The flag itself is not evidence; the ledger records and source documents behind it are. An exception becomes usable evidence when a reviewer has checked it against those records, documented the conclusion and kept the trail. Any AI statement that does not cite the underlying records should be rejected.

How often should continuous audit tests run?

Match frequency to how quickly the risk turns into loss. Payment and vendor master tests suit daily or before each payment run; journal entry and cut-off tests suit weekly runs and the month-end close. Running more often than a reviewer can clear the queue only creates backlog.
